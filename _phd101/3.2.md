Many junior PhD students believe the well-being risks described in the previous post will fade as they gain experience through the PhD journey, because the pressure should ease once they learn the skills, publish a few papers, and know how research works. I wish I could tell you that is true. What actually happens is that the nature of the pressure changes at every career stage, and each stage introduces new risks that are easy to miss precisely because you have already survived the previous ones.

**For Junior Researchers.** When you have little research experience or have not published at top-tier venues, the challenges that bother you the most are mostly about not knowing what to do, how to proceed, and whether you are working in the right direction. These challenges are unlikely to cause overconfidence, but they can cause you to push yourself too hard because you are not familiar with research, and more importantly, because you are not familiar with your own limits. The lack of experience means you cannot calibrate how much effort is appropriate and sustainable over months, and the flexibility of the PhD means no external structure will stop you from draining yourself day after day.

**For Experienced Researchers.** Once you have a number of papers published at top venues, your mental model shifts. For example, you no longer bump into as many concerns about research decisions, because you have started to gain successful, testable experience. You become much more confident in your ability to estimate how much time and effort a project will take, and that confidence introduces a new risk: overcommitment. You want to work on more projects at a time, because now you can see how each one could succeed, and you believe you can handle multiple ones concurrently.

One representative mistake made by experienced researchers is that they often stretch themselves at the edge of their capacity but barely leave any margin, draining their energy every day for the scheduled tasks. Everything may work fine if nothing unexpected happens; however, one unexpected event – a rejected paper (or a rebuttal that requires significant effort in adding experiments), a collaborator dropping out, a personal crisis – can push you from being stretched to completely burned out overnight, because there was no margin left.

**For Postdoc and Beyond.** Once you graduate and decide to pursue a research career, the responsibilities and opportunities both scale up to a new level. A postdoc often needs to allocate time and effort across multiple demands simultaneously: continuing to build a research reputation through first-author work, mentoring junior students, learning to write grants, and engaging in community services. The sudden rise in both opportunities and expectations can overwhelm a person who is used to managing a single research project at a time during their PhD training.

Becoming an assistant professor is another level entirely, where you handle mentorship, service, research, and funding all at once, in addition to the life responsibilities that tend to grow during these same years. The skills that got you through the PhD (e.g., deep focus on a single problem for months) are necessary at this stage, but they are no longer sufficient, because the coordination overhead alone can fill a week before you write a single line of your own research. Your role also transitions from being the person who does the actual work to being a manager who supports and coordinates the whole team.

I mention these later stages not to discourage you, but because knowing what risks and opportunities you are facing is the first step to managing them, and the earlier you build the habit of monitoring your own capacity, the better prepared you will be when the demands scale up.

### Knowing Your Limits

Burnout becomes difficult to avoid when you cannot accurately estimate your own limits, and the honest truth is that most people are bad at this. Your limits are not fixed, since they change with your health, sleep, stress level, personal circumstances, and the emotional weight of the work you are doing. A capacity that felt comfortable three months ago can become unsustainable after a difficult review cycle or a personal setback, so do not be frustrated if you reach your limit faster than you did a few months ago.

What matters most is how you calibrate your limits and plan reasonably to make sure you do not over-commit or leave too thin a margin.

The most practical approach I can offer is to pay attention to the early signals. When the quality of your writing drops and you find yourself staring at the same paragraph for an hour without progress, that is a signal. When you wake up already tired, and the thought of opening your laptop produces dread, that is a signal. When you become irritable with collaborators or withdraw from conversations you used to enjoy, that is a signal. None of these are the dramatic collapse that people picture when they think of burnout, but these signals arrive early enough that you can respond before the situation becomes severe.

Building awareness of your limits also means being honest about how many commitments you can sustain at one time. Every new project, every new collaboration, every service commitment adds weight, and you should estimate the workload based on the worst cases, because assuming everything goes smoothly leaves no margin for the unexpected. For instance, you clearly know when the reviews come out and how much time you have for rebuttal, so you should anticipate that, in the worst case, you will need to spend more than half of your time conducting the additional work for the rebuttal. Never push yourself to your limits, even if pushing harder might earn you one more publication in a given cycle, because one more paper does not matter if you can sustainably deliver high-quality publications every year.
