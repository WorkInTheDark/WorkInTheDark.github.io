Do you still remember the first semester of your college life? Everything feels novel, and you feel sharp. You take careful notes on every course, show up to every class, prepare each presentation a week in advance, and rehearse iteratively. You exercise regularly, eat properly, and go to bed at a reasonable hour, and more importantly, everything feels sustainable because the novelty of the work carries you through the effort.

By the third year, something has shifted. You are still functional, still producing, still showing up. But the notes are messier, the presentation slides get prepared a couple of hours before the presentation, and you are tired of revising the draft even though you know there are sections that are not ready. The exercise has quietly disappeared, and "a reasonable hour" now means something very different from what it meant in year one.

If someone showed you a side-by-side comparison of your work habits from the first semester and the current one, you would be surprised by the gap, because the change happened so gradually that you never noticed a single moment when you decided to lower the bar.

You did not decide to. **The middle did it for you.**

### The Long Middle

Every PhD has a clear beginning and a clear end. The beginning has orientation, coursework, a first (couple of) project(s), an advisor who is actively shaping your direction, and the electric feeling of entering something new. The end, on the other hand, has a defense date, a dissertation, a diploma, a job search, and the finish line in view. But what happens in the middle?

The middle has none of these. There are no scheduled milestones. Progress is invisible for months at a time. Unlike a course, in which the middle is often just two to three months, the middle of the PhD journey takes years, and the finish line feels too far away to generate any urgency in your mind despite the initial excitement having faded.

The students who struggle most severely during their PhD are rarely struggling in year one (when everything is new and structured) or in year five (when the end is in sight and adrenaline kicks in). They are struggling in year two, three, and four, when the work feels endless, the direction feels uncertain, every day feels identical, and the question "why am I doing this?" starts to feel less rhetorical and more genuine.

**The middle is where the majority of the damage happens, and almost no one warns you about it in advance.**

### What the Middle Feels Like

If you are in the middle right now, you may recognize some of these.

The excitement of a new idea, or the struggle of not knowing how to proceed, used to carry you through weeks of hard work; now, the feeling may last a day or two before the familiar execution sets in, and you kinda know how the project will evolve over the next couple of months, as well as your day-to-day work.

Still, your advisor gives you feedback; but instead of treating it as an opportunity to improve (as you did in year one), you start to feel exhausted at the thought of another round of revisions. You open your social media, swiping through your college friends posting promotions, vacations, and house purchases. You are still on the same stipend, still revising the same paper, and the gap that the peer pressure episode described is no longer an abstract concept. It is Tuesday night, you are staring at a paragraph that will not come together, and the thought arrives uninvited: "I don't know what I am persisting for."

Why do I know that? Frankly, what I just described happened to me hundreds of times during my second through third year, and I may even be understating it. I believe almost every PhD student I have worked with has had the same experience, including the ones who went on to extraordinary careers. The thought is a natural response to accumulated fatigue in an environment with no external structure to pace you. **The danger is making a permanent decision based on a temporary state.**

### The Fading Standard

The exhaustion does not just affect your motivation but also the quality of what you produce, and this is the part that concerns me the most.

You may check your citations every couple of days in your first year, verify every number and quote from the experiments, and re-read your own writing until it is as clean as you could make it. By the third or fourth year, you have seen and gone through enough of the process to feel like you know what "good enough" looks like.

**"Good enough" is a sign of danger, because it starts drifting lower without you noticing.** You send a draft with a figure you know is not quite right, thinking "my advisor will catch it and I can fix it later." You submit a paper with a methods section that glosses over a choice you cannot fully justify, thinking "the reviewers probably will not ask." Before submitting the paper, you skip the final pass to catch minor issues (e.g., grammatical errors, orphan words, bleeds) because you are tired and you tell yourself that "it doesn't matter much."

Each of these thoughts individually is small. Together, over months, they compound into a pattern where the standard you hold yourself to has quietly eroded from the one you started with. The same mechanism the wellbeing chapter describes later in this series applies here: just as the time you go to bed slides from 11pm to 2am in silent increments, **the bar for your professional standards drops in increments too small to notice**, until someone (your advisor, a harsh reviewer, or, in rare cases, your own honest self-reflection on a clear-headed day) points out that your current work does not match your earlier work.

### The Easier Path

The middle is also when the temptation of an easier path is strongest. Industry recruiters reach out with offers that pay five to ten times your stipend. A friend's startup is hiring and "would love someone with your skills." You start to think, "I could wrap up the current project quickly and grasp these opportunities."

First of all, none of these are bad options in themselves, and for some students, leaving the PhD or adjusting their ambitions is genuinely the right decision. The problem is that **the middle of the PhD is often the worst possible moment to make that call, because you are deciding from a state of accumulated exhaustion, not from a state of clarity.** The person who is tired of revisions, frustrated by slow progress, and wondering whether the whole thing was a mistake is not the person who should be making a decades-long career decision. It is like regretting a midnight impulse purchase, except the stakes are your career.

If you genuinely want to leave, give yourself a real rest first, which may take a couple of days or even weeks, and then ask the question again from a rested mind. If the answer is still "I want to leave," then leave with clarity and without regret. If the answer changes after rest, you will be grateful you did not act on the exhausted version.

### Getting Through

The saying that titles this post is a warning, and the warning has a practical implication: **the practices that got you through the beginning must be maintained deliberately through the middle, because the middle will not maintain them for you.**

**Check your standards explicitly, not when something breaks, but on a regular schedule.** Once a month, compare a recent piece of your work (a draft, a figure, an analysis) to something you produced a year ago. If the quality has dropped, it is a clear signal that you need to reconnect with the standard you set for yourself when you were fresh, and consciously bring it back.

**Create your own milestones when the program does not give you any.** The middle feels endless partly because nothing marks the passage of progress. But you could create milestones for yourself, for instance, a paper submitted, a chapter drafted, a new skill learned, a collaboration started, and checking them off provides the sense of forward movement that the middle otherwise lacks.

Besides, **protect the routines that sustain you** with the same deliberateness the wellbeing chapter asks for. If your exercise, your sleep, and your meals have drifted, correct them now, because every week you let the drift continue makes the middle harder.

And **talk to someone who has been through it**. Your advisor, a postdoc, or a junior faculty member. Ask them what their middle years felt like. You will almost always hear that they struggled too, and knowing that the feeling is normal and temporary is sometimes enough to keep going.

### 鲜克有终 | Few See the Journey Through.

Everyone begins well. The test of a PhD (and, if I am being honest, the test of most things worth doing) is whether you can **sustain the standard, the curiosity, and the care through the long, long middle when no one is watching and no external structure is holding you in place.** The students who finish well are not the ones who never got tired. They are the ones who got tired and kept the bar where they set it.
