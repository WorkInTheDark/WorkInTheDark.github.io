I want to start with a question to ask yourself.

**What am I learning from a PhD?**

Not surprisingly, many will say, "Such an easy question; I am learning to do research." Fair answer, but I insist you think more thoroughly about what you believe equips you to do research.

Now, I would expect many answers to emerge. Someone with an NLP background may say you should be familiar with the latest model fine-tuning techniques and the mathematical formulations behind them. Someone who runs human-subject studies may say you should know how to conduct interviews and qualitative analysis. Others may mention an endless list of research methodologies, such as participatory design, prototyping, statistical modeling, and so on.

This is a decent try. First, they are all valid answers, as you will definitely need these skills to finish each research project. Nevertheless, these skills are not what a PhD journey means, even though many people think a PhD is as simple as finishing several projects on the same topic and passing the defense.

Let me challenge this proposition first.

### Why Does a PhD Program Take So Many Years?

Today, in the AI era of 2026, an NLP project can be completed from scratch in as little as a month (and I am not even talking about the extremes), and many of you should have already heard that autonomous research AI systems can produce a reasonably competent paper from scratch in a couple of days.

So, if the PhD journey were just as simple as finishing several projects on the same topic, a hardworking human student should be able to earn the PhD in a year, and a hardworking AI could earn it in just a month. Obviously, this is not happening. In addition, an increasing number of prospective PhD students carry more than one first-authored, top-tier publication when they apply for the PhD program, and both numbers (the number of such students, and the average number of publications they have) keep rising. These students do not get the PhD degree after finishing one or two more publications.

Then, clearly, there must be something missing in your prior proposition: a PhD expects much more than just a handful of papers. What is that?

### Engineers vs. Scientists

Let me state my answer first, then explain it in detail: what you should aim to learn is a **methodology for thinking and learning**. You should not set the goal of "getting a paper published." Instead, the publications are just the natural outcomes that come alongside your path toward the goal.

Here is a simple comparison that explains the difference:

*"I know how to perform this study because I have done the same thing before"* versus

***"I know how to learn a new skill because I know how to search for and interpret relevant information, and translate that information into a skill I can develop myself."***

You can also think about the difference between these two narratives through the comparison between **engineers** and **scientists**. Engineers are trained to leverage existing knowledge and resources to make things work, and they are really good at doing so, which naturally maps to the first narrative. Scientists, on the other hand, are expected to explore unknown territory and discover new knowledge by (failing many times, learning from the failures, and) continuously asking why and how things happen. 

To clarify, scientists are not magicians or wizards who can predict the future or know the answer before they have ever tried. Nevertheless, scientists are those who know how to approach the answer, as well as how to learn from the unavoidable failures.

In industry, both types of roles are needed and irreplaceable. A top scientist could not deliver an engineer's tasks as well as a top engineer can, and vice versa. But the PhD program is training you to be equipped with the mindset of a scientist instead of an engineer, which is why merely knowing the answer to a problem you have encountered is not enough.

There is one question that many research assistants ask me while they are still hesitating about whether to apply for a PhD. They enjoy doing research, but they do not know what the PhD will actually bring back to them, or whether it is worth committing such a long period of time.

It is not surprising to see an undergraduate student who directly joins a company get promoted to a much higher rank than a new-grad PhD, and that undergraduate, having been in the industry for half a decade, is very acquainted with the workplace dynamics and their peers. You may wonder: a PhD normally receives a higher-ranked offer than an undergraduate, so why is this happening? Clearly, your undergraduate friend is also working hard while you are fighting with research problems, and they can get promoted quite fast if they are doing things really well. As a result, if your motivation for the PhD program is a higher salary, you will most likely be disappointed.

There is a second surprising truth that many people are not aware of, even after they finish the PhD. That is, almost every specific skill can, honestly, be learned faster in industry than during your PhD journey. You will typically have access to a pile of tutorials for the skills needed for your job, a mentor who is always accessible and who provides hands-on tutoring, and clear instructions for what you need to do every week.

But that is not what a good PhD produces.

### What a Good PhD Looks Like

You can actually identify what a good PhD graduate looks like quite easily. They may not be the person who finishes tasks the fastest, or who builds a product as polished as the top engineer who has spent a decade in the field. They may also not be the best manager or the most persuasive proposal writer.

But when a new challenge appears in a context where no one has a clear answer, many people will panic or avoid facing the issue. A good PhD graduate is usually the person who stays the calmest, who immediately starts to think about different possibilities, and who prepares for failures in advance. When something unexpected happens, they are the ones who immediately begin investigating the causes and planning mitigation strategies. 

A good PhD may not know the answer to the new question, but they know what approaches are likely to lead to a correct answer and how they should proceed. When they are communicating or conveying a perspective, for example to customers or to leadership, they are often more logically coherent, articulating their ideas with rationales and evidence. If there are critiques, concerns, or challenges from the management team, they will usually have a very clear and reasonable plan in mind for what to do and what the alternative strategies are if something unexpected happens, and their thoughts are typically quite comprehensive.

What makes this possible is not that they solved a similar problem before, but that they know the correct approach to investigate any problem, whether they have prior experience with it or not. After years of training, critiquing, and practicing critical thinking, they can objectively observe a situation, identify what is happening and what could be causing changes, discover the factors and underlying patterns beneath those observations, and arrive at an answer that holds up to scrutiny. These capacities do not expire when the technology changes, because they operate at a level above any specific tool, method, or context.

### The Moment You Realize You Have It

The shift often becomes visible when you start mentoring other students. When a junior student brings you a new research question in a domain you have never worked in, you will notice something surprising. Sometimes, you may not know the actual answer, but you know what the promising approaches are that could lead to a good answer. You can see the shape of the investigation, the kinds of evidence that would be needed, and the methodological choices that are likely to work, even though you have never studied the specific topic before.

You will also feel that many of the questions asked by junior students are somewhat "silly" to you now, because you know where to find the answers and would not need to ask someone else.

Your student, on the other hand, may be doing exactly what you were doing a few years earlier -- trying to get the answer, searching for someone who has already solved a similar problem, looking for a tutorial or a recipe. They are not yet aware that the methodology of thinking is far more durable and far more important than the specific answer they are struggling with. You were once in the same position, and the distance between where you were then and where you are now is not measured in publications, but in the quality of the questions you know to ask.

### The Practical Takeaway

This episode may sound like high-level philosophy, but it has a very practical suggestion for your day-to-day work. Whenever you are struggling with a problem or a specific challenge, do not just look for a solution. Find a way to understand why the solution works, why other approaches do not, and what the reasons are behind those differences.

The attempts you made that failed are not wasted time. Instead, they are a priceless treasure, because you will not have many periods in your life where making mistakes does not get you criticized or punished. What you should do whenever you encounter a failure is to understand why it failed, explore the reasoning behind the failure, and connect it to what you already know; those are the activities that build the methodology of thinking. The solution itself may never be useful again, because the next problem will always be different, but the understanding of why it worked will be useful forever, because the patterns of reasoning are generalizable.

This reminds me of a book I would strongly recommend: Charlie Munger's *Poor Charlie's Almanack*. Munger built his entire investment philosophy on learning from his own failures and from everyone else's failures throughout history, so that he could recognize the patterns that lead to unseen problems before they arrive. Similarily, the researcher who studies why things fail, including their own failed attempts, develops a quality of judgment that the researcher who only studies successes will never acquire.
