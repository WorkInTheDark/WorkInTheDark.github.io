Recovery from burnout is necessary when it happens, but the goal of your routine should be a rhythm of work and rest that prevents you from reaching that point in the first place. A few practices I have found effective, both for myself and for the students I work with:

**Protect your non-work time with the same seriousness you protect your deadlines.** If you block Tuesday evening for exercise or Saturday for family, treat that block as a commitment that research cannot override. The moment you start treating rest as the thing that gives way when work expands, the boundary dissolves and the invisible drifts from the first post begin again.

**Build transitions between work and rest.** The guilt of not working that we discussed in the first post is partly a boundary problem: when work ends and rest begins is unclear, so neither state is clean. A simple routine that marks the transition, even something as small as closing the laptop at a specific time and going for a walk, trains your mind to recognize that work is over for the day.

**Check in with yourself periodically, not just when something breaks.** Once a month, ask yourself honestly: am I sleeping enough, am I eating properly, am I moving my body, and am I spending time with people I care about? If the answer to any of these has quietly shifted, correct it before the drift accumulates further.

### Rest After the Deadline, and Before It

Researchers' calendars differ from the academic calendar because projects are mostly driven by deadlines, and research deadlines are semi-continuous. Paper submissions, revisions, grant deadlines, conference deadlines, and student milestones follow one another with barely a gap between them.

Many students would choose to defer taking a rest until "after this upcoming deadline." What in reality could happen is that they discover the next deadline is close enough and the rest window shrinks before the pressure rebuilds.

I want to be clear about both sides of this. Taking a genuine rest after a major deadline is **healthy and necessary**. After months of accumulated stress and effort, the relief of submitting a paper or finishing a grant is enormous, and you should use that moment to take a real break. You should spend time with people you care about and do something that has nothing to do with research.

I remember there were many times during my PhD that I stayed up all night to finalize and submit the paper (not a good habit, though), took a short nap, then jumped on the train to New York or the flight to Los Angeles later on the same day, and I would not open my work email for the whole duration of the trip.

The second side is equally important: you should also rest during the process, when you need it, and not only after the deadline arrives. If you defer all recovery to the deadline, you are asking your body and mind to run at full output for months with no restoration in between, and the quality of your work will decline long before you feel the burnout consciously. What matters the most is the change of mental model: that resting is not stolen time. Rest whenever needed, in whatever approach works best for you; for example, go for a city walk, take a genuine evening off, or develop some hobbies.

### The Complete Stop

There are always extreme situations when the signals of your well-being risks have accumulated to the point where your capacity to do meaningful work has genuinely broken down; the only effective response I know is a **complete stop**. To clarify, I do not mean working fewer hours or switching to a lighter task, because you are still in the research mental space, and the anxiety that brought you to this point continues to run in the background. I mean stopping entirely: closing the laptop, leaving the workspace, and disengaging from research for a period long enough for your nervous system to actually reset.

### Stop the Thinking Too

The part of a complete stop that most people miss, and that I want to emphasize as strongly as I can, is that **you need to stop thinking about the work, not just stop doing it**. When you are burned out and you close your laptop but spend the evening replaying the problems in your head, rehearsing what you should have written, or worrying about the deadline you are missing, the anxiety continues to build because rumination feeds it. Your body is resting, but your mind is still at the desk, and the recovery does not happen.

Stopping your thoughts about work is genuinely difficult, and I will not pretend it is something you can do by deciding to. What works is filling the time with activities absorbing enough to hold your full attention, so that the research thoughts cannot occupy the space. For me, traveling to a different city and exploring the food there works well, because the novelty of a different environment demands enough of my attention that my mind lets go of whatever I was stuck on. By the time I return, the problem often looks different, and the mental energy I need to address it has been restored. Your approach will be different from mine, but the principle is the same: **find something that fully absorbs your attention and gives your mind permission to be somewhere else for a while**.

### When to Seek Professional Help

Everything I have described so far is self-managed recovery, and for many situations, it is enough. There are situations where it is not, and recognizing the difference matters.

If the signals from the previous post persist for weeks after a genuine rest, if you find yourself unable to engage with work or life in ways that used to feel natural, if the anxiety or low mood has become a constant presence that does not lift with sleep or time away, or if you are having thoughts about harming yourself, these are signs that what you are experiencing has moved beyond what self-managed strategies can address. Seeking professional support from a counselor or therapist is the appropriate response, and doing so is a sign of self-awareness, not a sign of failure.

Most universities offer counseling services for graduate students, and many provide them at no additional cost. I encourage my students to learn what resources are available at their institution before they need them, because searching for help in the middle of a crisis is much harder than knowing where to go in advance.
