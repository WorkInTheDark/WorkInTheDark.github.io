From my own experience, the most productive and creative researchers I have met do not live a life that only has research. They typically have a lot of hobbies, and they are genuinely good at them. For instance, some are deeply into cuisine and can tell you how a specific regional dish was historically prepared, what separates a good version from a mediocre one, and why. Some are serious about investing, and the way they analyze a company makes you feel like you are talking to a professor in the business department or a senior trader on Wall Street. Some play instruments, train in martial arts, or build furniture. The commonality is not the hobby itself, but the way they approach it: you can feel the devotion, and you can tell they have learned how to develop their interests with real depth.

### Research Teaches You How to Learn Everything Else

If you think deeper about why these researchers are also so capable outside their field, there is a strong correlation with the research they do, and this is the key message of this episode. Learning to do research gives you more than paper publications, citations, and reputation. It teaches you the methodology of learning everything else.

Over the course of your PhD training, you will develop several capacities that extend far beyond the lab, and I want to name three of them here. The first is effective methodologies for learning new things: how to search for information, how to evaluate the quality of what you find, how to build understanding from the ground up, and how to identify when your knowledge has gaps. The second is a critical thinking habit that applies to everything you think and do: the ability to question assumptions, trace reasoning, identify evidence, and notice when an argument has a hole in it. The third is the self-awareness to know what genuinely interests you and what does not: the clarity to distinguish between something you are pursuing because you care about it and something you are pursuing because other people do.

These capacities do not stay within research. Once you have internalized them, you carry them into every new interest you pick up. The researchers who are remarkable outside their field are remarkable for exactly this reason: they can take their enthusiasm, time, and effort and invest it effectively into anything they care about, because they have learned how to learn. They are not developing these hobbies to show off among friends or because many other people do; instead, they are doing it because they genuinely want to understand, and they have the skills to go deep.

### What a PhD Is Actually Worth

If you ask me what I would expect of PhD graduates, the answer is definitely not a number of publications, citations, or academic achievements. From my perspective, those metrics are not as important as they appear if you view them across a lifelong time span.

Think about it yourself. If you choose not to pursue research as your career after graduation, does it matter at all how much you achieved during the PhD to your career in industry? And if you choose to stay in academic research, where you will eventually have hundreds of publications with ten or more adding to the list every year, does it really matter whether you had seven or ten during your PhD? Does it really matter whether you graduated with a hundred or five hundred citations?

What really matters is something much harder to measure: the awareness of knowing what you care about, what you enjoy, and what you do not. People are always busy doing things and rarely have the opportunity to think about why they are doing them and what they actually want to do. PhD training provides exactly that opportunity.

During the PhD, you are "protected" by the structure of the training itself. You do not need to worry about losing your job if you cannot deliver what your manager asks for next week. You are not navigating complicated office politics. You are not chasing unrealistic expectations of getting rich quickly, well, because the stipend does not give you that opportunity, and you do not receive stock refreshers. These pressures are constant stimuli for people in industry, but they are noise that PhD students are largely free from.

When you are free from that noise, you have what may be the best opportunity in your adult life to think about what you really want to do, what matters to you, why, and why not. That self-knowledge is the most durable thing you will take away from your PhD, and it will serve you in every career you pursue and every interest you develop for the rest of your life.
