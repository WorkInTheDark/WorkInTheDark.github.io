If you have never timed the actual time you spend on work every day before, I would suggest trying the following experiment.

Use any focus app that fires a timer and forbids you from using the phone during a pre-set period of time, focus on the research work during that period, and pause when time is up. Repeat it throughout the day and see how much time you have actually spent working. A simple timer on your phone would also work, but you need to stop it every time you check your phone, get water, respond to emails, or catch yourself staring at the screen without processing anything, and start it again only when you return to genuine concentration.

Most students who try this for the first time are shocked, because they sat at the desk for ten hours, but the timer says two and a half.

### The Energy Bar

I find the clearest way to understand this is to think of your daily cognitive capacity as an energy bar in a video game. If you have a good night of sleep, you wake up with a full bar, roughly equivalent to ten hours of normal activity. Every minute that passes costs you some energy, but different activities drain the bar at very different rates. And here is how the energy bar works:

1. Regular daily activities, such as commuting, eating, or casual conversation, as well as lightweight work tasks, like replying to emails and messaging your coworkers, drain the bar at roughly a 1:1 rate with real time.

2. Genuine research work drains the bar at three to four times the normal rate. It is because while you are doing genuine research, you are processing high volumes of information, critically analyzing what you read, generating hypotheses, evaluating evidence, and self-critiquing your own reasoning, all simultaneously. That level of cognitive intensity is not comparable to answering emails, attending a routine meeting, or writing a status update. As a result, 10 minutes of real research concentration consumes the same energy as 30 or 40 minutes of ordinary work.

3. Genuinely restful activities, such as watching something you enjoy, spending time with friends, or going for a walk, on the other hand, can replenish the energy bar at roughly a 1:1 rate.

Think about it for a second, what could happen? First, if you work on research continuously without rest, your energy bar drains in roughly three hours and you are done for the day. A better strategy obviously is to rest strategically in between focused sessions, as the rest periods partially refill the bar before you drain it again, and you can extend your total productive research time to four or five hours across the full day.

### The Illusion of the Ten-Hour Day

Once you see the energy bar, why "I can work for 10 hours a day" being an illusion becomes obvious. Even if you sit at the desk for ten hours, you are not concentrating for ten hours. You get water and snacks, go to the restroom, chat with someone, reply to a few emails, and browse something unrelated.

These interruptions are normal and unavoidable, and they are not the actual problem. **The problem is planning your day as if they do not exist.** When students estimate that they will make 10 hours of progress today and then fall short, they blame their discipline or their effort, or, as we've mentioned in Ch. 4 Ep. 1, they question whether they have not spent enough time on research, whereas the real issue is that the estimate was physically impossible from the start.

In fact, there has been quite a lot of scientific research supporting my arguments, as cognitive scientists found that top-level performers across multiple domains sustain high-intensity deliberate practice for approximately 3.5 to 4 hours per day, and gains diminish sharply beyond that. Economists documented the same diminishing returns in work output at higher weekly hours. Cognitive psychology experiments on sustained attention also show that accuracy and performance decline with time on task, and the decline starts earlier than most people expect.

Here's a clear takeaway. **You have approximately three to five hours of genuinely productive research time per day.** Everything you plan should be built around this number.

### How to Spend Your Time Well

Knowing the actual number will fundamentally change how you structure your day. The most important shift is recognizing that **your morning hours are your most valuable research hours**, because your energy bar is fullest and your capacity for deep thinking is at its peak. If you have a difficult section to write, a complex analysis to work through, or a hard conceptual problem to solve, protect the morning for it. Try not to start your day scrolling TikTok short videos, and push lightweight tasks after you finish the most demanding ones, because those lightweight tasks are still manageable when your energy is lower.

The second shift is learning to rest between concentrated work sessions so that you extend the total capacity across the day. I can share the strategy that works best for me.

My current strategy is to split every working chunk into 45 minutes of concentrated work followed by 10 minutes of genuine rest: stand up, stretch, get water, do something that is not cognitively demanding. Then I come back for another 45 minutes, or I move to a meeting. I never push past an hour of continuous concentration without a break. You may recall the timer advice from Ch. 4 Ep. 1 (set a timer for 40 to 60 minutes, then stand and move), and it serves double duty here: it protects your back and it protects your cognitive capacity.

The difference in output quality between a day organized this way and a day of unstructured marathon sitting is immediately visible. The structured day produces sharper thinking in fewer total hours, and you finish the day with energy left for the rest of your life.

### Why This Explains Late Nights

This framework also explains something Ch. 4 Ep. 1 described from the health perspective: why working late into the evening and past midnight is so inefficient. By evening, your energy bar is nearly empty. You are stretching yourself to stay at the desk, but the cognitive output per hour has dropped to a fraction of what it was in the morning. Many students spend an entire evening on a paragraph and produce work that was measurably worse than what they wrote in forty-five focused minutes the next morning after a full night of sleep, and I would recommend that they just go to sleep and continue the work the next morning.

Working late night is also not sustainable because you are also consuming the time that was reserved for a good sleep – otherwise, you won't get a full energy bar the next morning.
